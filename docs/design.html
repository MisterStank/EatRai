Product & Technical Design

EatRai “กินไร?” — so, what are we eating?

A location-aware restaurant app with a Tinder-style swipe deck. It learns your palate from every swipe, turns that into recommendations, and lets friends see how compatible their tastes really are before they argue about dinner.

01What's already in the market

The space splits into three clusters, and almost every product sits in exactly one of them. EatRai's bet is that the three belong together.

Cluster A — Group decision tools ("Chicken Tinder")

A host opens a room, friends join with a code, everyone swipes the same nearby list, and the app surfaces the restaurants everyone liked. The swiping is a voting mechanic, not a discovery habit — you use it for ten minutes and close it.

  • Food Swipe / Food Swiper, DineFinder, Dinn'r, Hungree, Tine, Sawa Eats, SwipeEats — near-identical: real-time room, anonymous yes/no, "it's a match" when the group converges.
  • Findalicious (formerly "Chicken Tinder") and SwipEats — open-source takes on the same idea.
  • Munch — added group scheduling / calendar on top of the vote.

Cluster B — Solo "what do I want" swipers

No social layer. You swipe meal photos or restaurants to shortcut the "I don't know, you pick" spiral for yourself.

  • Feed Me, Entrée, Tonight's Bite, Food Flip — swipe dishes/venues, get details on a right-swipe.

Cluster C — Taste profiles & ranked lists

Not swipe-first, but this is where the durable products live. They build a real model of your taste and keep you coming back to log meals.

  • Beli — the reference point. You rank a new restaurant by pairwise comparison against ones you've already been to ("which did you like more?"), producing a personal 0–10 score. It then computes a Match Score between you and each friend from how your rankings correlate, shows a blended "friends' rating" per restaurant, and recommends from all of it. Invite-driven, social, gamified with lists and streaks.
  • Paire (PersonalTaste™), TasteFit, TasteBuds, YumYumYo, Craive, DoorDash's Zesty — various "AI learns your palate" recommenders, mostly onboarding-quiz + feedback loop, little or no swipe UI.
  • World of Mouth — curated-by-experts guide; the anti-algorithm take.

What the market teaches us

  • Group-swipe apps have a retention problem. The loop is episodic and the switching cost is zero — a dozen of them exist because none of them stuck.
  • Beli's moat is the taste graph, not the UI. Pairwise ranking is clever but it's work; a lot of users bounce off the effort.
  • Nobody owns "swipe-native discovery that also builds a real taste profile and a social compatibility layer." That's the gap.

02Where EatRai fits

Keep the fun, zero-effort input method from Cluster A, and use it to build the durable taste graph from Cluster C — so the swipe isn't a throwaway vote, it's how you train the thing.

One-line positioning

“Swipe the restaurants around you. EatRai learns what you actually like, tells you where to go, and shows which friends you should never let pick dinner.”

keepSwipe deck, nearby-only, "it's a match" group rooms, invite-driven social graph
dropBeli-style pairwise ranking chore; onboarding taste quiz as the only signal
addEvery solo swipe updates a taste vector; compatibility is derived, not manually rated; recommendations rank the deck itself

Primary target user: 20–35, lives in or near a dense-ish city, eats out or orders in 3+ times a week, decides where to eat with other people regularly, already comfortable with swipe UIs. Secondary: travelers who want fast, taste-matched local picks without reading 40 reviews.

03Product — the six capabilities

  1. Swipe deck. Full-bleed photo card: name, price, cuisine tags, distance, open-now, and a "% your taste" badge once the profile exists. Right = like, left = pass, up = superlike (strong signal + saved to a shortlist), tap = detail sheet (menu, hours, map, friends who liked it).
  2. Right / left / super. Every swipe is an event: +1 / −1 / +2. Left swipes matter — they're negative training data, weighted lower than likes so a pass doesn't nuke a whole cuisine.
  3. Nearby browsing. Deck is always built from ST_DWithin(location, me, radius). Default 2 km, adjustable. No radius, no deck — this is a "where can I go now" product, not a wishlist of places across the world.
  4. Learns your taste. A 64-dim taste vector over cuisines + attributes, updated online on every swipe (details in §06). Cold start: a 5-tap "pick cuisines you love" seeds the vector; it's fully replaced by real swipe behavior within ~30 swipes.
  5. Recommendations. Not a separate feed — the deck is the recommender. Cards are ordered by cosine similarity between your taste vector and each restaurant's feature vector, lightly blended with external rating and freshness, using a pgvector index so it's one query.
  6. Friend compatibility. Add friends by invite/handle. For any pair we show a 0–100 score built from (a) taste-vector similarity and (b) observed agreement on restaurants both have swiped, blended by how much shared history exists (§07). Surfaces as "you + Mai: 82% — you both love northern Thai and neither of you can do sushi."

04Architecture

Deliberately boring. No microservices, no Kafka, no separate ML service — the "ML" is a 64-float dot product that runs inside Postgres. One deployable, one database, scales to a very large user base before any of this needs revisiting.

06Data model

Full DDL in backend/migrations/0001_init.up.sql. The shape:

taste_dimensions   append-only list; row order == index in every vector
users              handle, taste_vector vector(64), swipe_count
restaurants        place_id, location geography(Point), feature_vector vector(64),
                   cuisines[], price_level, rating, photo_urls[]
swipes             (user_id, restaurant_id) PK, direction {−1,1,2}, session_id?
friendships        a_id < b_id, status pending|accepted
compatibility      cached pair score: score, overlap_n, agree_n
sessions           code, center geography, radius_m, filters jsonb
session_members    (session_id, user_id)

Two indexes carry the product:

CREATE INDEX ON restaurants USING GIST (location);                    -- nearby
CREATE INDEX ON restaurants USING ivfflat (feature_vector vector_cosine_ops); -- taste

Taste space (the 64 dims)

Roughly 44 cuisine dims (thai_isaan, thai_central, sushi, ramen, neapolitan_pizza, smash_burger, szechuan, levantine, …) + 20 attribute dims (spicy, cheap_eats, fine_dining, healthy, cozy, trendy, late_night, good_for_groups, veg_forward, …). A restaurant's feature_vector is built once at sync time from its Places types/attributes/price; it's L2-normalized so cosine behaves.

07Taste-learning algorithm

Each user carries two vectors in the same 64-dim space, both updated in the request that handled the swipe — no training job, no model server.

coreAn online weighted centroid of everything you've reacted to. Slow-moving — "who you are." Liked places pull it toward them; passes nudge it away at ~⅓ the weight.
recentThe same signal as an exponential moving average with a ~25-swipe half-life. Fast-moving — "what you've been into lately."
 
// dir: +1 like, +2 superlike, −1 pass ;  w = dir>0 ? dir : −0.35
core'   = (core·n + feat·w) / (n + |w|)                  // incremental weighted mean
a       = 1 − exp(−ln2 / 25)                             // recent decay
recent' = (1−a)·recent + a·(feat · sign(w))
// recommendations rank against Blend(core, recent, 0.25) — mostly you, nudged by mood

Reference: backend/internal/taste/taste.go — UpdateCore, UpdateRecent, Blend. Tests show core aligning to a liked cuisine within a handful of swipes and recent tracking a short-term cuisine binge harder than core.

Why a centroid, not logistic regression or matrix factorization? At this scale there isn't enough per-user data for anything heavier to win, and a centroid is inspectable — we hand the user "your top dims are Isaan, Spicy, Cheap eats" and "lately: more Sushi, less Fine dining." Swap in a two-tower model later behind the same feature_vector interface once there's a swipe corpus worth training on.

The feature_vector for each restaurant is built once at sync time (taste.BuildRestaurantVector) from Places types, name, editorial summary, price level and attribute flags via a keyword→dimension map, then L2-normalized.

Recommendation query

SELECT r.*, ST_Distance(r.location, $me) AS dist
FROM restaurants r
WHERE ST_DWithin(r.location, $me, $radius)
  AND NOT EXISTS (SELECT 1 FROM swipes s
                  WHERE s.user_id = $uid AND s.restaurant_id = r.id)
ORDER BY 0.85 * (1 - (r.feature_vector <=> $taste))    -- cosine similarity
       + 0.15 * (COALESCE(r.rating,3.5) / 5)            -- light popularity prior
DESC
LIMIT 20;

In the build, PostGIS fetches ~120 nearby unseen candidates with their feature vectors and the deck package does the final scoring in Go — cosine fit + a light rating & distance prior + decaying early-stage exploration + a cuisine-diversity pass so you don't get five ramen shops in a row. New users (no vector) fall back to rating order. At larger scale the candidate fetch moves onto the pgvector IVFFlat index. Implemented in deck.RankSolo.

08Friend compatibility

Two signals, blended by confidence:

  1. Vector similarity — (cos(u_a, u_b) + 1) / 2. Always available, even for friends who've never swiped the same place.
  2. Observed agreement — of the restaurants both swiped, the fraction they swiped the same direction. Ground truth, but noisy when the overlap is small.
vecPart   = (cos(u_a, u_b) + 1) / 2
agreeRate = agree_n / overlap_n
conf      = min(0.85, sqrt(overlap_n) / 8)          // trust grows with shared history
score     = 100 * ( conf·agreeRate + (1−conf)·vecPart )

So new friends see a taste-profile estimate; friends with 40+ shared swipes see something that's ~85% driven by what actually happened. The basis field in the API response tells the client which regime you're in (taste-profile / blended / shared-history) so the copy can be honest. Implemented in Compatibility; recomputed on each swipe and cached in the compatibility table.

Beyond the number, taste.Agreements diffs the two vectors to generate the human line — the API returns bothLove: ["Isaan", "Spicy"] and neitherLikes: ["Fine dining"] so the friend row reads “82% — you both love Isaan & Spicy; you clash on Fine dining.”

09What makes EatRai different

A dozen group-swipe apps exist and none stuck (§01). Three deliberate departures:

a. Legible learning

Every card carries reasons — “Because you love Isaan · Spicy · Cheap eats” (taste.Explain: dims where both your taste and the restaurant score high). The Palate screen shows your top dimensions as confidence bars and your mood shift. The bet: if the profile is visibly paying off by swipe ~15, EatRai stops being a throwaway vote app. This is the anti-churn mechanism.

b. Consensus decks — async & min-regret

Every existing group app needs everyone in a live room and picks by average fit, which quietly steamrolls the outlier. EatRai's /v1/consensus takes any set of friends and ranks nearby restaurants by a maximin rule:

groupScore(r) = 0.7 · min over members of  (cos(memberVec, feat)+1)/2
              + 0.3 · mean of the same
              + 0.05 · (members who already liked r)
              = 0            if any member already passed r

So the deck surfaces places the least-happy member still likes — and it works off each person's stored taste vector, so nobody has to be online. A test asserts a restaurant one member would dislike can never top the group deck. (taste.ConsensusScore, deck.RankConsensus.)

c. Stretch pick + adventure streak

Pure matching engines never push you outward. Once you have a profile, one card per deck is a deliberate stretch: strong in dimensions you're weak on, but with a thread of overlap so it's a stretch and not a dart (taste.StretchScore). Accepting it extends an adventure_streak; passing resets it. Shown stretch picks are recorded so they don't repeat.

11Group sessions ("it's a match")

Two modes on one sessions table:

  1. Live — host creates a session, gets a 4-char code, friends join, everyone swipes the same deck ordered by the group's blended taste (via deck.RankConsensus so early cards already skew toward a match).
  2. Async — same, but the session has a quorum and a 24h expires_at; people swipe whenever.

On each right-swipe inside a session, db.CheckMatch runs one grouped query: has any restaurant reached quorum right-swipes with zero passes? If so the session closes with result_restaurant_id set. Clients currently learn this by polling GET /v1/sessions/{id}/state — cheap, no infra. A Redis pub/sub + WebSocket push is the obvious upgrade once live sessions are common. Session swipes still train the individual's taste vector (session_id is just tagged on the row).

Key risks

Cold deck in sparse areasNearby + unseen + good photos can run dry fast. Mitigate: expand radius automatically, allow "surprise me" beyond radius, seed launch in dense neighborhoods only.
Places API cost / ToSCaching place data has licensing limits (photo/attribution rules, refresh windows). Read the Places ToS before launch; budget a Foursquare/OSM fallback.
Retention (the Cluster A trap)If the taste profile isn't visibly paying off by swipe ~15, we're just another group-vote app. The per-card reasons and the Palate screen exist to make the learning legible early — this is the core product risk.
